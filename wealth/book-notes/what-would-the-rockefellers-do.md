# Book notes: What Would the Rockefellers Do?

Garrett Gunderson and Michael Isom. "How the wealthy get and stay that way, and how you can too."
Started 11/4/2026. Tim adds notes as he reads; Jarvis keeps this file.

## The big idea
Wealthy families stay wealthy for generations by running the family like a business and a bank:
- Write a family mission (what the money is for, what the family stands for).
- Teach kids money early, and pass down knowledge, not only cash.
- Treat people and skills (human capital) as the biggest asset, ahead of stocks.
- Keep a family "bank": the family lends to itself instead of to outside lenders.
- The book's tool for that bank is dividend-paying whole life insurance (often called "infinite banking").

## Keep (fits Tim's plan)
- Family mission and money lessons for his son, starting young. Ties to the son's accounts and plan.
- Skills and businesses as the main asset: DRT, Peace by Page, Legionz, Propa Pit.
- Borrow from yourself, not from Uplift, Flash Funds, Spotloan or Cash App loans.

## Be careful with
- The whole life insurance part is a sales pitch. Gunderson's company sells these policies.
- Premiums run about $200 to $500+ a month to fund one properly; agents earn large commissions.
- Cash value takes 5 to 10 years to build. Cancelling early loses money.
- Right now it would compete with rent and paying off high-interest loans.

## Tim's order of steps (Jarvis, 11/4)

3. Cheap term life insurance to protect his son.
4. Revisit whole life only once cash flow can carry the premium for 10+ years.

## Is it true? (Jarvis review, 11/4)
- Mindset parts (human capital, cash flow over net worth, family culture): mostly true. Real wealthy families use family offices, family meetings and money education. The Rockefellers ran a family office and set up trusts in 1934.
- "The Rockefellers got rich with whole life": stretched. Their wealth came from Standard Oil, then trusts, foundations and tax planning. Wealthy families mostly use life insurance to pay estate taxes. "Rockefeller method" is a marketing name.
- Whole life math: partly true, often misleading. You can borrow against cash value and it keeps earning, with legal protection and tax advantages. But loans charge interest, long-run returns run about 3 to 5% a year after 10+ years, early years are mostly fees and commissions, and many people cancel early and lose money.
- 401(k) criticism: half true. Fees and lockups are real, but an employer match is free money, which the book downplays.
- Use: family mission, money lessons for his son, building income streams. Skip whole life until the high-interest loans are gone and cash flow is steady.
