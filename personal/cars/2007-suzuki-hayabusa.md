## Condition from photos, 10/21/2026

Four photos: right side, left side, rear, front.

- **Paint:** candy red / maroon. The fairings look faded and close to matte; the tank and tail still have some shine.
- **Body damage:** scuffs and small cracks on the left and right side fairings and on the front fender. Check the mounting tabs behind each crack; broken tabs are common on Gen 1 fairings.
- **Decals:** Hayabusa kanji on both side fairings, GSX1300R on the tail.
- **Exhaust:** aftermarket short slip-on (single, right side) in place of the stock twin cans.
- **Controls:** spike-style bar-end weights.
- **Windscreen:** clear, aftermarket shape.
- **Wheels:** black stock 3-spoke wheels, both tires look usable from the photos (check date codes and tread).
- **Oil drip spots** on the concrete under the bike in two photos. Find the source before riding far. Most likely spots on a Gen 1, in order:
  1. **Countershaft (front sprocket) seal.** Remove the sprocket cover; oil on the sprocket and chain means this seal. Cheap part ($15-40), 1-2 hours DIY.
  2. **Clutch slave cylinder** (left side, behind the sprocket cover). Gen 1 slave seals leak clutch fluid (brake fluid), which also eats paint. Rebuild kit $30-80, new slave $100-180.
  3. **Oil cooler lines and O-rings** (front of the engine, under the fairing). Wet lines or fittings mean O-rings or a line. $40-200.
  4. Also check: oil pressure switch, drain plug crush washer, oil filter, valve cover gasket, and fork seals (oil on the fork tubes).
  Clean the engine, ride 10 minutes, park on cardboard, and look for the first wet spot.

## Build plan

Order of work: **1. Mechanical → 2. Stretch and lowering → 3. Chrome → 4. Paint → 5. Audio.**
Mechanical first so the bike is safe. Stretch and lowering next because they change the swingarm, wheel, chain and fender fit. Chrome before paint so the plated parts go on a finished bike without scratching new paint during swaps. Audio last because the pods are fiberglassed into the painted fairings and tail.

### 1. Mechanical

- Fix the oil drips (see above).
- Full service: oil and filter, coolant, brake and clutch fluid, plugs, air filter, chain and sprockets check.
- **Regulator/rectifier:** the stock Gen 1 unit runs hot and fails; swap to a MOSFET unit (Shindengen FH020AA type) before adding audio.
- Battery test; replace if weak.
- Braided stainless brake lines (standard on every build), new pads, front tire if it's old.

### 2. Stretch, 300 fat tire, lowering

**300 fat tire kit.** An extended swingarm plus an 18 x 10.5 inch rear wheel and a 300/35-18 rear tire. Gen 1 kits come from Hayabusa-specific shops and builders: Fat Tire Kits, Ricky Gadson, Brock's Performance (swingarms and wheels), Vance & Hines, and similar. A full kit includes the swingarm, wheel, rotor and sprocket carrier, spacers, axle, longer chain, offset front sprocket, and rear brake line extension.

- **Chain alignment:** the 300 tire pushes the chain line out. Kits handle it with an **offset front sprocket** (moves the chain out about 1 inch) or a **wide-tire conversion** (jackshaft or offset carrier). An offset sprocket is simpler; check chain-to-tire clearance with the bike on a stand before the first ride.
- **Belt or chain:** chain is the normal setup on Busa fat tire kits. Belt drive conversions exist on some custom kits; they are quieter and cleaner but cost more, and replacement belts are special-order. Stay with chain unless the kit maker offers a proven belt setup.
- **Stretch length** (inches added over stock):
  - **2-4 inches:** handles close to stock, easy in parking lots, still turns well.
  - **6 inches:** common street stretch. Slower steering, wider U-turns, less wheelie on launches. Good balance for a street show bike.
  - **8-10 inches and up:** looks the most dramatic, best for straight lines and shows. Slow to turn, wide turning radius, harder to back out of a parking spot, and the long chassis drags on driveways and speed bumps once it's lowered.
  - The 300 tire itself makes the bike want to stand up in turns; it needs more countersteer. Ride it easy for the first few hundred miles.
- **Decision for Tim:** pick the stretch (suggest 6 inches for street use) before buying the kit, since the swingarm is made to length.

**Lowering.**

- **Rear:** lowering links (dog bones), 1-3 inches. Cheap and bolt-on.
- **Front:** slide the forks up in the triple clamps (about 1 inch) or an internal fork lowering kit (2-3 inches). Keep the front and rear drop matched so handling stays neutral.
- **Kickstand:** shorten it (cut and weld, or buy a shorter one) so the bike doesn't stand too upright and tip.
- **Air ride option:** rear air shock with a compressor, small tank and a handlebar switch. Drops the bike to the ground when parked and raises it to ride. Costs more and adds wiring; not in the base total.
- Lowering cuts lean angle and ground clearance. Watch the exhaust and the bottom fairing on driveways.

### 3. Chrome

Parts: front and rear wheels, extended swingarm, levers, mirrors, bar ends, foot pegs and rearsets, triple clamp cover, trim bolts. Frame rails are optional (the frame is aluminum; plating it means stripping the bike down to the bare frame).

Three ways to get the look:

| Method | Look | Durability | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Real chrome plating** (copper, nickel, chrome) | True mirror finish | Best if done well; aluminum parts can pit or peel if the shop rushes prep | Highest. Wheels $400-1,000 a pair, swingarm $600-1,200. Takes 2-6 weeks |
| **Powder coat "chrome"** (super chrome powder) | Bright, close to chrome at a distance, less mirror | Scratches easier, can dull outdoors; not for parts that get hot | About 40-60% of plating |
| **Hydro dip chrome film** | Looks chrome on small parts | Weakest; peels with heat, brake dust and fuel | Cheapest; small trim only |

Recommendation: real chrome on the wheels and swingarm (order the kit swingarm and wheel chromed from the maker, which is often cheaper than plating later), bought chrome aftermarket levers, mirrors and small parts, and skip hydro dip on anything that sees heat or road grime. Polished aluminum is another option for the wheels; it looks bright but needs regular polishing.

### 4. Paint: Harley Quinn inspired

**Theme (from Tim's reference picture, described here instead of saved):** red and blue, black and gold diamond harlequin pattern, gold pinstripes, and maybe "LOVE" / "HATE" style lettering.

**Chosen direction (Tim, 10/21): option B refined.**
- Show-quality candy red fading to candy blue (front to rear), not a hard split.
- Smoky black diamond (harlequin) pattern airbrushed into the fade, not hard-edged.
- More gold: gold pinstripes and gold leaf accents, gold brake calipers.
- Chrome as planned (wheels, swingarm, trim), stretched with the 300 rear tire.
- Red and blue LED underglow.
- **Emblems:** Suzuki emblems replaced with a custom diamond emblem: four quadrants in red and black with a gold outline (tank and fairing badge locations).
- Tim's favorite reference builds: the white and red flames chrome stretched Busa, and the candy red chrome Busa with red underglow.
- Candy plus gold leaf plus airbrush is the top of the custom paint range; plan on the high end of the paint line and get 2-3 quotes from custom painters with motorcycle candy work in their portfolio.

- Fairings: the current ones are cracked and scuffed. Plastic welding and tab repair works for small cracks. A new aftermarket ABS Gen 1 fairing kit ($600-1,200) is often cheaper to paint than repairing worn plastics, and the old set stays as a spare.
- Paint options: custom painter (best result for diamonds, pinstripes and lettering), vinyl wrap with printed graphics (cheaper, removable), or DIY.
- **Character name and logos:** fine on a personal bike. If Tim ever sells merch with the bike, or shows it commercially (paid shows, sponsored content), skip the official Harley Quinn name and DC logos and keep the theme generic (harlequin diamonds, red and blue, playing-card style lettering).

### 5. Audio

**Speakers:**
- **Front inner fairing panels by the gauges** (the "A-pillars"): two 3.5 or 4 inch speakers in custom pods.
- **Undertail:** two 5.25 or 6.5 inch speakers in a fabricated undertail enclosure.
- Use marine or motorcycle-rated speakers (water and UV resistant): Rockford Fosgate (PM marine line, Harley-fit line), Hogtunes, Kicker (KM marine).

**Amp and control:**
- A small 2 or 4 channel marine/powersports amp (Rockford Fosgate PBR series, Kicker KEY or similar).
- Bluetooth controller with a handlebar remote, or a compact waterproof head unit.
- Waterproofing: sealed connectors, heat-shrink, drain holes in the pods, and the amp mounted under the seat away from exhaust heat.

**Fabricated pods:** fiberglass or 3D-printed pods molded into the inner fairing and tail, then painted with the bike.

**Charging (Gen 1 limit):** the stock stator makes about 400 W, and most of it runs the fuel injection, fan, lights and ignition. At idle and low rpm there is little spare output. A loud amp can pull 10-25 A, which drains the battery at stoplights.
- Test the stator output (AC volts between the three yellow wires at 5,000 rpm, about 60-70 V each pair) and charging voltage (13.8-14.5 V at the battery).
- Options: a higher-output aftermarket stator plus the MOSFET regulator; a second small battery for the amp on an isolator/relay so the starting battery stays full; or a lithium battery. Plan at least one of these before installing an amp over about 100 W RMS.
